Variant readings

Six plays carry a critical apparatus: 604 entries, each after the word it is about, listing which witness reads what. The old site showed the edited text with an asterisk that opened the readings. Playcode currently pastes the readings into the line, so La discreta enamorada, v. 72 reads “mucho la hermosura llama llamafalta infama ,”. We plan to keep only the edited text in the line and make each entry a note of its own kind, “Textual variant”.

The full background, with the six plays and examples: Variant readings and HTML fragments in the TEI files.

1

Should a variant be marked with a number, like the notes, or with an asterisk as before?

The old site marked a variant with an asterisk and listed it apart from the notes, which carry numbers. Playcode numbers the notes 1, 2, 3 through the play. A variant could take the next number, so a page has one sequence and one list, or keep a sign of its own.

Answer

2

Should readers see Hamlet’s variant readings?

Hamlet’s text mostly follows the Second Quarto (Q2, 1604). Its TEI file records 90 places where the First Quarto (Q1, 1603) or the Folio (F1, 1623) reads differently, as at v. 19: Q2 “ho! Who is”, F1 “who’s”, Q1 “who goes”. Most are small: at v. 8, “Barnardo.” where F1 reads “Barnardo?”. (The file counts 94 entries, but 4 are copies sitting inside the editor’s notes.)

The old site showed 4 of the 90, at v. 1319 and vv. 3256–3257. The other 86 never appeared: their lines were printed with no marker, so readers never knew they were there.

They look unfinished. 81 of the 90 sit in the first 313 lines (Act 1, scenes 1 and 2) of a play of about 3,900. Some are damaged: at v. 189 the entry for “an … a” was put after every letter “a” in the line, so it would appear four times, three of them in the middle of a word; at v. 313 (“too too sullied”) the entry is empty and the readings were typed into a note instead; three readings name no witness.

Whatever you choose, the entries stay in the TEI file. This is only about what readers of the site see.

Answer

3

How will readers know what a siglum such as “P3” or “H” stands for?

In a variant, each reading is labelled with the editor’s siglum. La discreta enamorada, v. 72, reads “– H llama – P3 falta – MP infama”. A reader who does not know the edition cannot tell what H, P3 or MP are. The six plays use 42 sigla between them.

They name two kinds of source. 21 are early printings or manuscripts, the play’s witnesses: P3 is the Parte tercera de comedias de los mejores ingenios de España (Madrid, 1653), MP a printing of 1735. The other 21 are modern editions from the play’s bibliography: H is Hartzenbusch’s Comedias escogidas (1860). Playcode now holds all 42, taken from FileMaker, so every siglum points at a known witness or edition.

The old site printed the sigla and nothing more. Its list of witnesses and its bibliography did not show them, so a reader had no way to match “P3” to a book. Playcode’s play page does the same today.

The second and third answers can go together; say so in the notes.

Answer

4

Should readers see the variants by default?

The reading pages have switches for line numbers, stage directions and asides. Variants could be shown with a switch to hide them, hidden with a switch to show them, or always shown.

HTML typed into the text

Seven plays hold 1,211 fragments of HTML written as text inside the TEI. The old site printed them as HTML, and its stylesheet hid one kind. Playcode stores them as text, so 236 lines show the tags themselves. Whatever you choose, these tags will no longer reach the page as text.

Background and examples: HTML typed into the text.

5

What should readers of Hamlet see of the words found only in the Folio?

Hamlet’s text follows the Second Quarto (Q2, 1604). The editor also added the words that only the Folio (F1, 1623) prints, each passage between two superscript F’s: 247 passages over 155 lines, from a single word to runs of 14 lines. At v. 316, Q2 reads “O God, God,” and the Folio “O God, O God,”.

The old site hid them by default, so its text was Q2’s. A switch in its side panel, “Folio interpolations: Show”, brought them back in grey between their F’s. Playcode today shows the code itself: “O God, <span class="folio"> <sup> F </sup> O …”.

Answer

6

How should the Q4 Additions of The Spanish Tragedy be marked?

The Additions are the five passages first printed in the fourth quarto (Q4, 1602). They run from a few lines to a whole scene: the Painter scene is about 130 lines and 45 speeches. All four versions of the play, English, French, Italian and Spanish, mark where each one starts and ends with a superscript “Q4”. The English and Italian versions also set the Additions in italics; the French and Spanish do not, although the Spanish translator’s notes say they are printed “con un tipo de letra distinto”.

The marks were typed as pieces of HTML, and on the old site several went wrong. Most ends are written backwards, so the closing “Q4” came out full size. In the French version, one backwards end set the rest of a paragraph in superscript (“Bon voyage, Messieurs !…”). In the Spanish version, Additions 2 to 5 were wrapped in the code that hid Hamlet’s Folio text, so the first line of each disappeared, together with its “Q4” and the translator’s note announcing it. Playcode today shows the code itself: “<sup> Q4 </sup> [Draws his sword.] </sup> Q4 <sup>”.

Whatever you choose, we mend the broken marks and every line is shown. The question is how a reader sees where an Addition begins and ends, especially a long one.

Answer

7

Should readers of The Rover see the 40 short stage directions the old site hid?

In The Rover, 40 short stage directions are wrapped in the same code as Hamlet’s Folio-only words (question 5), so the old site hid them; unlike Hamlet’s page, this one had no switch to show them. They are “Aside” (28 times, and once “Aside to Hellena”), “within” (5 times: a character speaking from offstage), “Exit” (twice, closing a scene), “To Hellena” and “To Willmore” (who a speech is addressed to), and two names inside entrances: “Enter Antonio and his page Diego” and “… and Belvile’s page Boy”.

Readers lost little with the asides, because the aside itself is set in its own style. The rest was simply missing: the old site printed “Enter Antonio and his page ; Antonio knocks on the hilt of his sword”, and nothing told the reader that Belvile’s lines at the door are spoken from offstage. Playcode today shows the code itself around them.

The play has the same kinds of direction elsewhere, shown normally: “(Aside)” 53 times, “(To Willmore)” 4 times, “Exit” 5 times. We do not know why these 40 were set apart. The edition’s note says Summers’ additions to the 1677 quarto are in brackets and the digital editor’s in double brackets; these have neither.

8

Is v. 623 of Mariamne “Devant le Ciel voûté, trône du Dieu qui tonne,”?

A letter is missing from this line in the file. It reads “tr”, then a piece of HTML that starts superscript, then “ne”. The tag stands exactly where an “ô” would be, so the letter seems to have turned into the tag when the file was made. On the old site the rest of the line, “ne du Dieu qui tonne,”, came out in superscript; Playcode today shows the tag as text.

Elsewhere the play spells the word “trône” (vv. 208, 466, 835) and “thrône” (vv. 8, 387). What is left here, “tr…ne”, fits “trône”, or the old spelling “trosne”, which the play never uses. The text follows Alan Howe’s edition (Exeter, 1989), which would settle it.

Line breaks and prose line numbers

Verse needs no line breaks: each verse line stands on its own, with its own number. Prose is one block per speech, and the TEI files mark a new line inside it with <lb/>. They use it in four ways. The old site turned every one into a new line. Playcode currently turns every one into a space, and leaves them out of the TEI file it exports; we will keep them in the file whatever you choose.

9

How should prose line numbers be shown?

40 plays number their prose as well as their verse: Hamlet, The Way of the World, Endymion, The Malcontent and King Lear among them. Each number marks where a printed line of the source edition starts, and the count runs through verse and prose alike, so every line of the play has an address: Hamlet’s “Beggar that I am, I am ever poor in thanks” is line 1319.

Playcode loses these numbers when it imports a file, so readers see the verse numbers jump. In Hamlet 2.2, verse line 1208 is followed by prose, and the next number shown is 1454: some 245 lines with nothing to cite them by. The TEI file Playcode exports loses them too. We will keep the numbers whatever you choose; the question is only how readers see them. They would follow the “Line numbers” switch, as verse numbers do.

10

Which line breaks without a number should readers see?

Most of them set something apart: a letter’s greeting, a song or verse quoted in a speech, a new line after a stage direction, the lines of a dedication, and, with an empty line, a dedication’s signature. Running them on, as Playcode does now, joins lines that were meant to stand apart: “…como deseo. Frey Lope Félix de Vega Carpio”.

In eight plays they do something else. They copy where each line ended in the book the text was taken from, mid-sentence, like the numbered breaks of question 9 but with no numbers: Romeo y Julieta (3,390), Le Soir des rois (1,658), Le Mardi gras du cordonnier (1,334), Enrique IV, primera parte (961), The Shoemaker’s Holiday (784), La trágica historia del Doctor Fausto (277), The Duchess of Malfi (242) and The Spanish Tragedy (79). The old site showed these too, so Capuleto’s speech came out in the book’s short lines: “Y demasiado pronto ajadas están / aquellas que tan precozmente han sido / madres.” A few dedications do the same, Congreve’s in The Way of the World among them. In these eight plays a song or a letter may hide among the book’s line ends; only reading them would tell.

Historical performances

Next on the list is bringing over FileMaker’s historical performances, which emothe.uv.es prints under “Representaciones antiguas”: company, venue, date, cast, venue type, a note and the information source. FileMaker holds 317 performances, 243 of them on 59 plays Playcode publishes.

  • FileMaker keeps a source record for nearly every volume and page cited: the 252 performances that cite a source point at 104 records, which hold about 16 works. Wiggins’s British Drama 1533–1642 alone is 81 records, cited 152 times, under ten spellings of its title (“1532-1642”, “1533+1642”, “A Catalgue”…); Mora Sena’s Restoration Comedy, 1671–1682 is 7 records under five spellings of its authors.
  • The pages never reached readers. 74 of those 81 Wiggins records hold the page, but the old site printed only the volume and year: “Wiggins, Martin. British Drama 1533-1642: A Catalogue. Volume 5: 1603-1608. 2015.”
  • Missing details show as FileMaker’s placeholders: 55 published performances read “{Falta año pub}” or the like.
  • CATCOM is cited 46 times, under two records, and its licence asks for its credit line wherever it is cited. The old site printed the credit once, typed into a venue’s name.
  • The performance’s place is not in the tables we have; it is in the request at the end of this page.
11

Where should a performance’s source be recorded?

Take the 39 performances that cite volume 5 of Wiggins: FileMaker reaches it through 15 separate records. There are three ways to hold such citations:

1. As a work in the bibliography. British Drama 1533–1642 becomes one entry, and each performance adds its own volume and page (“vol. 5, p. 330”), as a play’s bibliography already says where the play sits in a modern edition. A correction to the entry reaches every performance that cites it, the citation prints the same everywhere, and the pages finally show.

2. As a separate list of sources, built the same way. Performances stay apart from the bibliography, but a work cited by both is entered and corrected twice.

3. As free text on each performance, as FileMaker prints it. Simplest, but a correction to Wiggins means editing each of the 152 citations by hand.

The same FileMaker table also gives the sources of 365 witnesses (DEEP, Greg’s Bibliography, ESTC…), which are not imported yet; whatever you choose here would serve them too. We recommend the first.

What should these thirteen kinds of note be called?

Five kinds of note have a name: translator’s, editor’s, critical editor’s, digital editor’s and author’s, and a note with no kind shows as a plain “Note”. Thirteen more kinds in the files show as a plain “Note” too, and the Notes page’s filter lumps them together. Counts are from the 370 files.

They are of three sorts. falta_tipo means “type missing”: the kind was never chosen. 100 of its 132 notes are glosses of old French words in Félismène, from Furetière and Nicot; the rest are notes by the editor or translator of nine other plays. Nine kinds name a subject rather than who wrote the note, and come almost all from Friar Bacon and Friar Bungay, its Spanish version and Hamlet. Three are slips.

Verse, prose or both

Every EMOTHE file labels its play verse, prose or both, on the line that gives its size: <extent ana="verso">2749 versos</extent>, ana="prosa", or ana="versoprosa" for verse and prose. Playcode reads only the number. It works the form out from the text instead, where one numbered verse line makes a play verse, and only a person in Playcode can call a play “verse and prose”. So the 41 plays labelled verse and prose show as verse, and the files Playcode writes label every play verse, a prose play as “0 versos”.

The full background, with the counts and the plays where label and text disagree: Verse, prose or both in the TEI files.

22

Where should a file say whether its play is in verse, prose or both?

Today the files say it on extent, the size of the text, as an ana attribute. TEI accepts that, but it is the project’s own convention: extent is meant for a size, and ana for a pointer to an interpretation.

The alternative is TEI’s own place for classifying a text, textClass, where the files already give their CDU number. The three forms are declared once in the file’s header, and the play points at one of them: <catRef scheme="#forma" target="#versoprosa"/>. Other TEI projects, and tools that read TEI, look for a classification there. extent then keeps only the count, “2749 versos”.

23

When a file’s label and its text disagree, which should Playcode follow?

They disagree on 91 plays. 33 labelled prose hold a song or a few quoted lines of verse, so Playcode calls them verse: the Italian Giulio Cesare (EMOTHE0252), in prose but for 3 lines, shows as “3 verses”. 17 labelled verse have no numbered verse line, so Playcode calls them prose: La tragedia del vengador (EMOTHE0279). And the 41 labelled verse and prose show as verse. The 45 files that count speeches and the 8 with no label say nothing either way; Playcode would go on working their form out from the text.

Working it out from the text today, Playcode counts only verse lines that carry a number, so 17 plays whose verse lines have none show as prose, among them As You Like It (1,235 verse lines). Whichever answer you give, the rule will count every verse line; how much verse makes a play verse, or “verse and prose”, is part of this question: 13 of the 17 are prose plays with a few songs or quoted lines.

Answer

Verse identifiers

Every verse line in the files carries an identifier of its own beside its number: <l xml:id="em0010-en-0001" n="3-4">Who’s there?</l>, the first line of Hamlet. There are two shapes: “em”, the play’s EMOTHE number, the language and a running number (em0010-en-0001, 239 plays), or the language and a running number alone (es-0001, 113 plays). The running number counts the lines in the order of the file, and no two lines of a play share one. Playcode reads them and writes them back unchanged, and shows them in its editor without letting anyone change them.

The number of a line cannot do this job. A verse split between speakers is one number on two or three lines (9,851 numbers are shared this way), 59,347 verse lines have no number at all (songs, plays whose lines are not numbered), and some numbers follow a printed edition (“3-4” in Hamlet). The identifier names exactly one line. Within the files only Hamlet’s variant readings point at lines by it (<app to="#em0010-en-0136">, 11 readings); outside them, anyone may use it to link to or cite a line of a published file. Nothing in Playcode or on the site uses it.

What identifier should a verse line added in Playcode get?

Today it gets none, and the file Playcode writes gives that line none. Playcode could give it the next free number in the play’s own shape (es-3679 in a play of 3,678 lines): never used before, so no link breaks, but out of order when the line is added in the first act. Numbering every line again in order would keep the running number true, but every link to a line after the new one would then point at the wrong line.
