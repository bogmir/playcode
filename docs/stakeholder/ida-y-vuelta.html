Where each part of a play goes

A play in Playcode reaches six places. This page shows which parts of a play reach which, where something is lost on the way, above all between a play and its TEI file, and which parts are not in Playcode yet.

Prepared by Bogdan. The round trip was measured on 11 October 2026 on the 394 plays of the development copy; the imported data was counted on production’s 373 plays on 12 October, and the parts the TEI re-import brought in recounted after it on 15 October. Updated on 13 and 14 October: the cast list’s heading, a dating given only in words and the historical time now come back, and the published site prints the cast list’s own heading and, with the downloads, the closing formulas. The downloads also print the date of composition and the historical time. The files turned out to label each play verse, prose or both already; Playcode ignores the label, so where it belongs is now a question for the team. The exported file carries the CDU number again; the witnesses come back from it whole, with their line breaks; a translation’s file names its original, so the two are linked again whichever is imported first; the bibliography comes back from the file, with each play’s own place in an edition; a digital editor comes back as one, not as an editor; two italic passages side by side come back as two; the kind of a stage direction can be corrected in Playcode; the notes on the edition are read from the file and written back; where the digital text came from is read, written back and printed; the actors of the original cast are read and written back; and the scene of the action is read, written back and printed after the cast list; and italics outside the text, in the sources, dedications, introductions and cast lists, are printed and written back as italics, where until now readers saw “<<El prado de Valencia>>”. Updated on 15 October: every text printed around the play, a paratext (a dedication, an introduction, a licence to print, an epilogue), keeps the kind its file gives it, where 285 had become a plain “note”, and its line breaks; those after the text are read, written back and printed after it; editors can put them in order. The files carry no FileMaker or Playcode record numbers. Updated on 16 October: the HTML typed into the text is gone from three plays, The Rover, Mariamne and La tragedia española; the line breaks of prose, letters and songs and the numbers of prose's printed lines are kept, shown and written back (questions 9 and 10); and the variant readings of six plays are read, written back and shown as asterisks that open the readings, each witness named, with a switch to hide them and a setting to hide a whole play, which Hamlet has until its editor has finished (questions 1 to 4). Updated on 17 October: Hamlet’s Folio passages are read, written back and shown behind a switch, off until a reader turns it on (questions 5, 36 and 38).

Why the TEI file matters

The TEI file is meant to be a complete copy of a play. It is published with the site as the edition's long-term archive, and it is how a play moves: a backup restored, a play loaded into another installation. If a play is exported and its file imported into an empty Playcode, everything should come back, but for the four things kept out on purpose (see Into the TEI).

So the rule is: if TEI has a way to say it, it goes into the file, wherever it came from: the TEI file itself, a form in Playcode, or FileMaker. FileMaker’s data was imported once; whatever of it lives only in Playcode’s database cannot be recovered from the TEI files. That is why the table says where each part’s data came from.

To check, every play was exported and its file imported again as a new play, then the two were compared field by field. The text comes back whole. Several things around it do not.

Importing a file over the play it came from is a different case. That keeps what the team has set in Playcode (whether the play is complete, its family, its historical time) and everything brought in from FileMaker, so it loses much less. What it replaces is the text itself.

Notes

  1. The downloads print the title, author, editors, sources, the date of composition and the historical time, dedications and cast list; the EPUB also the original title and the licence. The publication details, the project and editorial statements and the identifiers are not printed: whether they should be is question 24.
  2. The file names the original by its code, the part before the first “_” (EMOTHE0530), and Playcode links the two whichever is imported first: a translation that arrives before its original waits for it, and an original typed in by hand counts too. An original already chosen in Playcode is never replaced. All 153 links come back. Since 18 October 2026 the kind comes back too, from the main title’s type, as the files write it: a translation, an adaptation (ten plays), a refundición (none yet) or a version (three plays, question 40).
  3. Kept out of the file on purpose. Whether a play is complete is Playcode’s own switch for what visitors see, not part of the edition: every file on the published site is a complete play, and a play imported from a file arrives as a draft, to be checked before visitors see it. A whole installation is restored from a backup of its database, not from the files.
  4. The years are written as numbers the file can be searched by, the words beside them as they were entered. A dating given only in words, such as “posterior 1590 y anterior 1595”, is written as words alone (18 plays).
  5. The period and its explanatory note are both written: 109 plays, 38 of them with a note.
  6. The EMOTHE files already label their form, on the extent that counts their verses: ana="verso" in 222 files, "prosa" in 56 and "versoprosa", verse and prose, in 41; 45 count speeches ("intervenciones") instead, and 8 say nothing. Playcode reads only the number and works the form out from the text, where one numbered verse line makes a play verse: the 41 plays labelled verse and prose show as verse, and so do 33 labelled prose that hold a song or a few quoted lines, while 17 labelled verse show as prose. The file Playcode writes labels every play verse, a prose play as “0 versos”. The downloads print the form worked out from the text, never one set by hand. No play has its form set by hand yet. Where the label belongs, and whether it or the text decides, are questions 22 and 23; the background is Verse, prose or both in the TEI files.
  7. They come back marked as coming from the file, whatever their origin, each in the role it had.
  8. All 510 witnesses of 107 plays come back, with the line breaks in their titles and notes (45 fields in 8 plays); a double space comes back as one, which is all any page prints. They come back as the file’s: the file names no FileMaker record (see Into the TEI).
  9. The bibliography comes back from the file: all 2,804 references of 115 plays, each with the play’s own volume and pages in it. A reference several plays cite comes back as the one shared reference, recognised by its description, since the file carries no FileMaker or Playcode number; so FileMaker’s word-for-word copies of one work become one (72 references into 32). Importing a file only adds what Playcode does not have: it never changes or removes a reference, so a correction made in Playcode stays, and before an import adds any reference Playcode lists them, for a new play too, so an older file’s version of a corrected reference can be caught. The researchers’ private notes are never written, on purpose, because the file is published with the site.
  10. Checked by an automated test. No play in the development copy has places yet, so the measurement could not count them. FileMaker records the places of the action of its plays (138 links to about 94 places), still to be imported.
  11. Playcode does not record which list a character is in, so the plays with a cast list per act or part (La Tragédie espagnole, ’Tis Pity She’s a Whore, El galán Castrucho, Le Malade imaginaire) come back from the file with a single list, under the first heading; the play page and the downloads print each list with its heading, but every one lists all the characters, and the published site prints one list.
  12. The kind of a stage direction (entrance, exit, stage business…) is chosen in the content editor from the list the TEI guidelines suggest; a kind a file gives outside the list is kept. Some kinds in the files are wrong, such as “Sale don Pedro solo.” marked as an exit, and can now be corrected. A verse’s identifier, the file’s name for the line (em0010-en-0001), is shown there but not changed by hand, so that no two lines share one; a line added in Playcode gets none, and what it should get is question 25. The spelling delivery_ in 24 files is read as the TEI’s delivery.
  13. Every italic passage comes back as it was, two side by side included. An italic with no words in it, left in three notes, is not written: there is nothing in it to keep.
  14. Worked out from the text each time, so not part of the file.
  15. Imported into the development copy from FileMaker (510 witnesses on 107 plays), and a trial run on production found the same plays. The import itself has not been run on production yet.
  16. Until 15 October production had its notes, and the stage directions inside verses, pasted into the lines, as the first import left them; the re-import of every TEI file that day moved them out.
  17. The CDU number of the source files follows the play’s language one for one: 821.134.2-2 for Spanish (151 files), 821.133-2 for French (84), 821.111-2 for English (82), 821.131-2 for Italian (45); the 8 Portuguese plays leave it empty. Playcode holds the language, so it loses nothing by not reading the number, and writes it back from the language.
  18. The play page and the published site show it; whether the downloads should print it too is question 24, with the other parts they leave out.
  19. A note on the edition as a whole, in the file’s header: “El epílogo figura en la edición de Ferrara de 1581, que no es la edición princeps” (L’Aminta). Some are remarks for readers, four list the witnesses and editions used, and three are an editor’s reminders (“¡¡¡CUIDADO, REVISAR QUE SÍ HAY APARTES QUE NO SE MARCAN!!!”). emothe.uv.es shows none of them, so Playcode keeps them in its play form and in the file only; whether readers should see them is question 26. Production’s plays got theirs from the re-import of 15 October.
  20. “Procedencia” on emothe.uv.es, which prints it under the source on the pages of its older layout: “Procedencia: IntraText Digital Library” for L’Aminta, Folger Digital Texts, Internet Shakespeare Editions; its newer pages leave it out. Playcode prints it under the source. 61 of the 152 hold no real value: “Texto base” (59 plays, Hamlet among them) or “ProcedenciaFuente” (2), and one more file gives an empty one. Playcode keeps them in the file but prints none of them; what “Texto base” means is question 27. Production’s plays got theirs from the re-import of 15 October.
  21. The actors of the first performance, in the play’s own cast list: The Way of the World (1700) gives twelve, “Fainall, in love with Mrs. Marwood: Mr. Betterton” among them. They are edited in the content editor’s cast list. emothe.uv.es prints none of them, so Playcode keeps them in the file only; whether readers should see them is question 28. Two more files use the place for something else: El sueño de una noche de verano puts a description of five roles there, kept as the file has it, and A Midsummer Night’s Dream leaves one empty, which is dropped. Production’s play got them from the re-import of 15 October.
  22. Printed after the cast list, where emothe.uv.es prints it and 15 of the 17 files put it; Egle’s, which its file puts before the cast list, moves there too. emothe.uv.es leaves out its heading (“SCENE:”, “The Scene”); Playcode prints it. Two scenes are wholly in italics (Timone d’Atene, Enrico IV, parte seconda), and are printed so; the note in Il cavaliere del pestello ardente’s scene is left out, like the other notes of the front matter. 44 more files give the scene as a section of its own kind (circunstancia_accion), right after the cast list; since 15 October it is read as the scene of the action and written back as one. Production’s plays got theirs from the re-import of 15 October.
  23. Each paratext keeps the kind its file gives it: an epistle, an address to the reader, a licence to print, a title page, a note on the digital edition… Until 15 October any kind outside five was read as a plain “note”, which 285 had become; production’s plays got their kinds back from the re-import of 15 October. A few kinds in the files are wrong, five of them listed under “Kinds to correct”, and are corrected in the content editor. Every line break comes back; an empty line, before a signature in 140 plays, parts two paragraphs. A paratext may be a heading alone (12 title headings). The 17 paratexts after the text, in 13 plays (The Rover’s epilogue, Everyman’s appendices, licences and colophons), are read, written back after the text and before the bibliography, and printed after the text, each with an entry in the play page’s navigation and the published site’s Contents. Editors put the paratexts in order by dragging them.
  24. Since 16 October, in a file that marks no Folio passage the Folio wrappers are dropped and their words kept: The Rover’s 40 stage directions read “Enter Antonio and his page Diego”, and the wrappers round four Additions of La tragedia española are gone. Mariamne (EMOTHE0239) reads “trône” at v. 623, corrected in our copy of its file. Since 17 October Playcode reads Hamlet’s 132 Folio passages (119 inside one line, paragraph or stage direction, 13 across several): readers see them only when a “Folio interpolations” switch is on, in grey between small F’s, on the play’s page and on the published site; the downloads, which have no switch, print them in grey (question 38); and the file Playcode writes carries them (question 36: the 119 as proposed, the 13 in the file’s own form until question 37 is settled). The content editor shows a passage as <folio>…</folio> around its words, with a hint on how to type one. Hamlet was re-imported on production and the site generated on 17 October. One mark in Hamlet is unclear (question 39). The Q4 Additions of The Spanish Tragedy and its translations still show their tags; they wait on question 37, which the editors are taking to the TEI Guidelines.
  25. Playcode keeps every line break and every printed line's number since 16 October; production's plays get them when their files are next re-imported, and the published site when it is next generated.
  26. A line break is typed with Enter in the text's box; a printed line's number as <lb n="1320"/>, where that line starts. A paragraph holding a break opens in the full form, never the one-line quick edit, so no break is lost.
  27. Playcode keeps the 542 entries of the six plays since 16 October; the files hold 604, but 62 are copies, inside a note, of an entry already in the line, and are read as that entry. Production’s plays get them when their files are next re-imported, and the published site when it is next generated; the numbers of those plays’ notes go down then, since the readings’ notes are no longer counted. Hamlet’s variants are kept and written back but hidden from readers, by the play’s “Show variant readings” setting.

Four things stay out of the file on purpose: whether a play is complete or a draft, which is Playcode’s own switch for what visitors see; the researchers’ private notes, because the file is published with the site; the statistics, which are worked out from the text; and the record numbers of FileMaker and of Playcode, which belong to an installation, not to the edition. The only numbers a file gives are play codes, such as EMOTHE0530 for the original of a translation.

What happens next

  1. Write the rest into the file, as proposed in Into the TEI: a play’s several cast lists (the kind of an adaptation or a refundición has been written as the main title’s type since 18 October 2026). The parts still to come from FileMaker (performances, genre, places) go into the file from the start.
  2. An automated check that exports a play with every part filled in, imports it into an empty Playcode and compares, so that nothing added later can be lost the same way.

Neither needs a decision from the team. The parts not in Playcode yet, at the end of the table, are projects of their own: the HTML in the text, the performances, the form, verse or prose, and what the downloads print wait on the team’s answers in the questions for the project, and the genre on FileMaker’s genre table.
